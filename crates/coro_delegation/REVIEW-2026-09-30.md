# Adversarial review of the coroutine-executor lock study (2026-09-30)

Scope: `crates/coro_delegation` (executor, locks, workload, stats, `FINDINGS.md`,
`paper/paper.typ`) and `crates/coro_tokio_baseline`. Method: code reading,
re-analysis of the checked-in result JSONs, and a small side experiment
(details and provenance in §Experiment; raw JSONs, patches and runner scripts
in `.worktree/review-2026-09-30/`, never in `results/`). Nothing under
`crates/coro_delegation/` other than this file was changed.

Severity: **invalidates-claim** (a headline number or sentence is wrong as
stated), **weakens-claim** (true on this workload, but the paper generalises
beyond what was measured), **presentation**.

## The three things we missed

1. **The 3.9–5.6 k-cycle "scheduler round trip" is manufactured by the
   workload, not by the executor.** Every client does its parallel work as a
   synchronous spin *in the same poll* that released the lock, and every
   worker also hosts an always-runnable spinning bystander. So a dispatched
   grantee is queued behind at least one 4 000-cycle spin (the unlocker's
   parallel work if wake is inline/LIFO) or behind a 1 000-cycle bystander
   poll plus the FIFO queue (default placement). With one `yield_now()` between
   `run().await` and the parallel spin (the natural shape of real async code:
   `lock().await; ...; some_io.await`), `dispatch` with inline hand-off goes
   from 0.234 to 0.368 Mops/s and its non-CS cost per op `o` from 4 570 to
   **1 112 cycles: the same as `ces-k64-home` (1 105) and `fc-remote` (974)**.
   Fair `dispatch-pq` with inline hand-off goes from 0.316 to 0.554 Mops/s at
   Jain 1.000, i.e. **0.91× FC-PQ's ops/s and 0.86× its utilisation, not the
   paper's ~0.5×**. On real tokio the same one-line change lifts
   `tokio::sync::Mutex` from 0.235 to 0.349 Mops/s sustained and from 0.072 to
   0.323 bursty, which erases most of the 1.5–1.6× sustained and all of the
   "LIFO hand-off delay" bursty advantage claimed in Q4/§2.4. The paper's Q3
   summary ("a dispatched hand-off costs 4.2–5.4× FC-PQ's per-op lock cost")
   and the intro's "delegation is the fast realisation" are conclusions about
   the harness's post-unlock spin, not about hand-off versus delegation. This is
   the `ces-pq` the paper lists as untested; it took a 4-line change
   (`Placement::Inline` is already in the executor and deliberately mapped to
   `Default` in `dispatch.rs:259-262` and `dispatch_pq.rs:326-329`).

2. **"Equal cumulative lock time" is the wrong fairness goal for a
   closed-loop, heterogeneous, *and* intermittent population, and the paper's
   own model shows why.** Usage order is enforced on *cumulative* charged
   cycles with no decay. A client that is absent (I/O, idle, a newly arrived
   tenant) keeps its low counter and, on return, is served ahead of everyone
   until it has caught up: measured, a light client active 20 % of the time
   still receives **1.00× the lock time of an always-present light client
   under `dispatch-pq`** (it does 5× the ops per active second, and its p50
   run latency is 9 µs against its peers' 164–700 µs p99) and 0.38× under
   FC-PQ (`c0` and `c16` identical: the clamp does not cap the *advantage*, only
   the wait). FIFO gives it 0.19×, i.e. proportional to presence. The paper
   presents Jain over the whole run as the fairness figure of merit; with any
   arrival/departure pattern that number rewards a policy that starves
   steady clients on behalf of returning ones. SCL's lock slices exist to
   bound the accounting horizon (the paper calls the omission "no lock
   slices" in Related Work without noting what it costs). The claims "service
   Jain 1.000" and G1 "clients receive equal lock time" hold only for the
   closed-loop always-present population of the harness.

3. **The throughput/utilisation comparison across locks is not apples to
   apples, and the paper's own "utilisation identity" hides the reason.**
   Usage ordering changes the served mix from 1:1 to ~5.5:1 light:heavy, which
   halves the mean CS cost (`C̄` 4 900 → 2 460 cycles). Ops/s then rises 1.4–2.9×
   (FC-PQ "2.89× dispatch") purely by serving cheaper work; the paper concedes
   this in Q6 but still leads Q3/Q4/the abstract with ops/s ratios, and
   utilisation (`CS / T`) counts the *spin inside the closure* as useful work.
   Since the CS is 1 000/8 000 TSC-timed spin cycles plus one BTreeMap insert
   (~300–400 cycles), ≥ 75 % of "utilisation" is a `while rdtscp() < end`
   loop that no locality, prefetching or combiner effect can change. The
   right like-for-like measure is *cycles of lock overhead per operation at
   the same mix* (the `o` column), or throughput at a fixed target mix; on
   that measure the story of §1 above collapses the gap between hand-off and
   delegation to noise once the harness artefact is removed.

## Ranked issues

### I1 — invalidates-claim: the dispatched hand-off cost is a harness artefact

**Claim affected.** Paper §1 ("A hand-off that wakes the grantee through the
scheduler spends 3 885–5 612 cycles between grant and CS start, 4.2–5.4×
FC-PQ's whole per-op lock cost, so fair `dispatch-pq` runs at 0.43–0.54×
FC-PQ's utilisation"); Q3 (`sec:qdel`) in full; Q4 sustained ratios
1.5–1.6×; conclusion "delegation is the fast realisation we measured";
FINDINGS 2026-09-29 dispatch-pq (c), (d).

**Evidence.**

- `workload.rs:333-347`: `client.run(...).await; ... spin_cycles(cfg.parallel_work_cycles)`.
  For `dispatch`/`dispatch-pq` the future returns `Ready` synchronously from
  `critical()` (`dispatch.rs:335-347`), so the release, the hand-off wake and
  the 4 000-cycle parallel spin are all inside one poll of the unlocker.
  With `Default` placement the grantee goes to the *back* of the unlocker's
  local FIFO (`executor.rs:377-384`), behind the bystander (1 000-cycle poll)
  and any other queued clients; with the LIFO/inline slot it waits behind the
  unlocker's own 4 000-cycle spin. Either way "grant→start" is dominated by
  workload spin, not by scheduler mechanics. The instrumented JSONs agree:
  `dpqi-dispatch-pq-home-c256-w8-h8-b31-sus-r1.json` grant→start = 2.30 G
  cycles over 591 608 hand-offs = 3 885 cycles, roughly one parallel spin.
- `dispatch.rs:259-262` and `dispatch_pq.rs:326-329` map
  `Placement::Inline` to `Default`, so the executor's own inline slot, the
  exact analogue of a CES resume, was never tried for a hand-off mutex. The
  paper calls this `ces-pq` and marks it `todo`; it is a placement flag.
- Side experiment (W = 8, 64 clients, heavy 8×, b31, 1 s window after 200 ms
  warm-up, n = 2, medians; same machine state as the 09-29 window, CPUs 0–15 at
  3.0 GHz; scratch binary sha256 `6abd913c…`, patch in
  `.worktree/review-2026-09-30/coro_delegation-review.patch`):

  | variant | parallel = spin (paper) | parallel = yield-then-spin | parallel = sleep (timer, no CPU) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | | Mops/s / util / o (cycles) | Mops/s / util / o | Mops/s / util / o |
  | `dispatch` | 0.211 / 0.478 / 5 448 | 0.215 / 0.488 / 5 249 | 0.258 / 0.582 / 3 571 |
  | `dispatch-inline` (new) | 0.234 / 0.514 / 4 570 | **0.368 / 0.814 / 1 112** | 0.299 / 0.662 / 2 487 |
  | `dispatch-home` | 0.239 / 0.554 / 4 100 | 0.234 / 0.541 / 4 328 | 0.247 / 0.568 / 3 843 |
  | `dpq-c256` | 0.266 / 0.300 / 5 790 | 0.266 / 0.301 / 5 784 | 0.350 / 0.389 / 3 839 |
  | `dpq-inline-c256` (new) | 0.316 / 0.329 / 4 683 | **0.554 / 0.582 / 1 660** | 0.428 / 0.448 / 2 836 |
  | `dpq-home-c256` | 0.308 / 0.364 / 4 543 | 0.305 / 0.358 / 4 621 | 0.321 / 0.378 / 4 255 |
  | `ces-k64-home` | 0.365 / 0.821 / 1 081 | 0.363 / 0.818 / 1 105 | 0.358 / 0.809 / 1 172 |
  | `fc-remote` | 0.375 / 0.838 / 951 | 0.374 / 0.834 / 974 | 0.369 / 0.825 / 1 042 |
  | `fcpq-h16-home-c16` | 0.609 / 0.681 / 1 151 | 0.609 / 0.680 / 1 157 | 0.587 / 0.657 / 1 288 |

  Spreads are ≤ 0.004 Mops/s on every cell except `dpq-inline-c256` yield
  (0.550–0.557). The paper's numbers (0.212 / 0.311 / 0.378 / 0.612) reproduce
  in the "spin" column to within 1 %, so the scratch build is not a
  different binary story. Service Jain is 1.000 for every `dpq-*` cell and
  0.997 for FC-PQ in all three columns; no client or bystander starved.

- Real tokio, same change (`tokio::task::yield_now().await` before the
  parallel spin; scratch `tokio-bench` sha256 `4cdf1181…`, patch in
  `.worktree/review-2026-09-30/coro_tokio_baseline-review.patch`):

  | cell | spin (paper) | yield-then-spin |
  |---|---|---|
  | `tokio-mutex` W8 sustained | 0.235 Mops/s, util 0.536 | **0.349, util 0.791** |
  | `tokio-mutex` W8 bursty | 0.072, util 0.167 | **0.323, util 0.732** |
  | `std-mutex` W8 sustained | 0.211, 56 starved clients, all bystanders censored | 0.209, 0 starved, Jain 0.53, bystander p99 1.7 ms |

  The last row shows that the *worker seizure* form (§2.3, "the first $W$
  clients keep the $W$ workers for the whole run") is also a
  never-yielding-loop artefact: a blocking mutex still blocks the worker
  while it waits, which is the real cost, but the whole-run seizure and the
  56 starved clients come from client tasks that never return `Pending`.

  The bursty 3.4–3.7× "LIFO hand-off delay" of §2.4 and Fig. 1(c) is the
  unlocker's synchronous 32 000-cycle spin sitting in front of the LIFO slot.
  With a yield, LIFO-on tokio (0.323) exceeds the paper's LIFO-off number
  (0.263) and is 1.08× `fc-remote` bursty (0.298 → 0.291 with yield).
  Sustained, `ces-k64-home`/`fc-remote` beat yielding `tokio-mutex` by
  1.04–1.07×, not 1.5–1.6×.

**Why this is not a nit.** The paper's causal story ("every fair decision
costs a scheduler round trip on the critical path") is stated as a property of
hand-off locks in cooperative executors. What was measured is: a hand-off lock
whose grantee is placed *behind the unlocker's own CPU-bound continuation*
loses to a lock whose successor runs *before* that continuation. That is a
statement about where the unlocker's post-CS work sits relative to the wake,
i.e. about the LIFO/inline placement choice, which the paper already frames as
one of its five subversions (§2.4). Inline hand-off (`ces-pq`) is the
obvious fix and it is one flag away.

**Fix.** (a) Un-map `Placement::Inline` in `dispatch` and `dispatch-pq`, add
`-inline` to the labels, and run the 09-29 dispatch-pq matrix with it (it is
`ces-pq` with chain bound = ∞; add the `ces` chain bound + home break to it
for the G3 comparison). (b) Add a `--parallel-mode {spin,yield,sleep}` knob to
both harnesses and report every headline at least for spin and yield, with the
spin column labelled "synchronous continuation, worst case for LIFO/inline
placement". (c) Rewrite §1/Q3/Q4: the round-trip cost is 1.1–1.7 k cycles when
the continuation yields, comparable to a combining pass's admin; the
delegation advantage that survives is ≤ 10 % sustained and is inside the
executor's own `home` vs `remote` variance. (d) Drop "delegation is the fast
realisation" unless it survives (a)+(b).

### I2 — invalidates-claim: cumulative usage ordering rewards absence; Jain over the run cannot see it

**Claim affected.** G1 ("clients receive equal lock time"), "service Jain
1.000 behind a hand-off mutex and 0.997 inside a combining lock", the
Related-Work sentence that dispatch-pq "is, in effect, [u-SCL's] async analogue
(usage order and a starvation clamp, no lock slices)".

**Evidence.**

- `fc_pq.rs:398-416` (`push`): the key is `n.usage()`, the client's lifetime
  cumulative charge; `dispatch_pq` likewise charges `base + cs` with no window
  or decay. A client that pauses keeps its old, now comparatively tiny,
  counter. The clamp (`promote_starving`, `fc_pq.rs:362-384`) only *lowers*
  keys of long waiters; nothing raises the key of a returning client.
- Side experiment (`int4-*`): W = 8, b31, sustained, clients 0–3 (2 light, 2
  heavy) active for one 50 ms slot in five, all others as in the paper; 1.5 s
  window, n = 2. Service (and ops) of an intermittent client relative to the
  mean always-present client of its class:

  | lock | light intermittent | heavy intermittent | run-latency p50 of the intermittent client vs steady peers' p99 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | `fc-remote` (FIFO) | 0.19× | 0.19× | 164 µs vs 179 µs |
  | `fcpq-h16-home-c16` | 0.38× | **1.00×** | 25–29 µs vs 330 / 598 µs |
  | `fcpq-h16-home-c0` | 0.37× | **1.00×** | 25–29 µs vs 328 / 596 µs |
  | `dpq-home-c256` | **1.01×** | **1.01×** | 9 / 45 µs vs 698 / 923 µs |
  | `dpq-inline-c256` | **1.00×** | **1.00×** | 9 / 55 µs vs 660 / 894 µs |

  Run-level service Jain is 1.000 (`dpq`) and 0.984 (FC-PQ) in those runs, so
  the paper's metric reports these as *fairer* than FIFO while a client present
  20 % of the time gets the whole lock-time share of a client present 100 % of
  the time, at 20–70× lower latency, taken from the steady clients.
- SCL (Patel et al. 2020) bounds the accounting horizon with lock slices and a
  penalty relative to the other *active* contenders; CFL (Park & Eom 2024)
  schedules lock occupation with a CFS-style clock [both from memory of the
  papers' design sections; verify the exact windowing rule before citing].
  Neither, as far as I recall, orders by an unbounded lifetime counter.

**Fix.** Either (a) window/decay the usage counter (e.g. exponential decay per
tick, or SCL's slice + window) and re-run the fairness cells, or (b) restate G1
as "equal lock time among *continuously contending* clients" and add the
intermittent-client result as a limitation. (a) is what a reviewer will ask
for; it also interacts with I3 because a windowed clock changes the served mix.
Add an intermittent/arrival scenario to the harness either way (the
`--intermittent N` knob in the review patch is a starting point).

### I3 — weakens-claim: ops/s and "utilisation" are not like-for-like across locks that serve different mixes, and utilisation counts the spin

**Claim affected.** Q3 "fair dispatch-pq completes 1.27–1.51× dispatch's
ops/s"; Q4 "beats `tokio::sync::Mutex` by 1.58–1.63×" (fc/ces at the FIFO
mix; the fair lock's 2.79–3.28× ratio to std-mutex is quoted in FINDINGS);
Q6's util identity; every `×dispatch` column in FINDINGS.

**Evidence.**

- `summarize_dispatch_pq.py:12-15`, `make_tables.py:52-55`: `util = Σ harness
  service_cycles / (measured_secs × tsc_hz)`; `service_cycles` is `cycles()`
  around `insert + spin_cycles(cost)` (`workload.rs:334-339`). With costs of
  1 000 / 8 000 cycles and an insert of ~300–400 (`tab-fifo.typ`: `CS_L` =
  1 280–1 404), the spin is 71–78 % of a light CS and 95–96 % of a heavy one.
  A `while rdtscp() < end` loop is unaffected by cache state, so no lock can
  change `C̄`; the only lever a lock has on util is `o`. The paper's Q6 says
  "the per-op lock cost `o` accounts for all of the loss" as if it were a
  finding; it is arithmetic given a TSC-timed CS.
- `C̄` moves from ~4 900 (FIFO mix) to ~2 460 (fair mix). Reporting FC-PQ as
  "2.89× dispatch" in ops/s (FINDINGS l.177) and `dispatch-pq` as "1.27–1.51×
  dispatch" mixes a 2× change in work per op with lock cost. The paper knows
  this (§3, Q6) but the abstract-level numbers and Fig. 5/7 still use ops/s.
- Not stated anywhere: the TSC-spin CS means *locality* (the standard argument
  for delegation: the combiner keeps the data structure hot) cannot
  contribute. The BTreeMap of 65 536 `u64` keys is about 2 MB [INFERENCE:
  ~8 keys per 200-byte leaf at 70 % fill], i.e. one core's L2 on this Xeon
  and trivially L3-resident; an insert touches a handful of cache lines. The
  whole FC/CES advantage in this study is scheduling, not locality, and the
  paper never says so.

**Fix.** Report cost per operation at a *fixed* mix as the primary
efficiency metric: `o` (cycles/op not in CS), and throughput for the FIFO
mix (fc/ces vs dispatch/tokio) and for the fair mix (fcpq vs dispatch-pq)
separately; never a fair-vs-FIFO ops/s ratio without the mix. State explicitly
that the CS is a TSC spin, so locality effects are excluded by construction,
and add one real-work CS variant (e.g. insert + 8 random lookups, no spin) to
show the direction of the locality term.

### I4 — weakens-claim: always-runnable spinning bystanders and the balancing steal are load-bearing executor choices

**Claim affected.** Q1 (burden fairness, bystander p99 "within one bucket"),
§2.5 (CES "traps" 5 clients at b0; "with balancing the bystander is stolen
during warm-up and never returns"), Q5 (actor is burden-fair "only because
balancing steals keep moving the server"), and the tokio comparison (tokio has
no balancing steal; FINDINGS caveat 3).

**Evidence.**

- `workload.rs:355-360`: bystanders `spin; yield_now()` forever. `executor.rs`
  docs (l.45-50) say balancing was *added because* steal-when-idle never fires
  with such bystanders. So the executor's most consequential policy exists to
  cope with a harness artefact. tokio does not have it, and with tokio's
  defer-list semantics for `yield_now` the same bystander *does* let a worker
  count as idle (FINDINGS l.18-22 of the tokio workload docs).
- Side experiment, sleep/sleep world (parallel work and bystander work both
  spent as a timer wait; every task is `Pending` most of the time; b31 unless
  stated):

  | variant | Mops/s | util | o | worst bystander p99 | burden Jain |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | `dispatch` | 0.235 | 0.558 | 4 131 | 16 µs | – |
  | `dispatch-inline` | 0.325 | 0.716 | 1 922 | 9 µs | – |
  | `dispatch-home` | **0.134** | 0.328 | 11 051 | 13 µs | – |
  | `dpq-inline-c256` | **0.492** | 0.506 | 2 211 | 6 µs | – |
  | `dpq-home-c256` | **0.145** | 0.204 | 12 069 | 13 µs | – |
  | `ces-k64-home` | 0.214 | 0.494 | 5 208 | 14 µs | 0.997 |
  | `fc-remote` | 0.225 | 0.512 | 4 773 | **238 µs** | 0.996 |
  | `fcpq-h16-home-c16` | 0.296 | 0.353 | 4 809 | **100 µs** | 0.983 |
  | b0 `fc-remote` | 0.222 | 0.506 | 4 888 | 238 µs | – |
  | b0 `dpq-inline-c256` | 0.488 | 0.507 | 2 220 | 9 µs | – |

  With idle workers that park, `fc-remote`'s injector wake costs a futex
  round trip per served waiter (64–69 k parks per worker per second in
  `fc-remote-sleep-sleep-r1.json`), the combiner is 0.40× its spin-world
  throughput and its bystander p99 is 238 µs; `home`-placed wakes (the paper's
  recommended placement for both `ces` and `fcpq`) hit a parked home worker
  and are the slowest variants. The paper's placement recommendations
  (`fc-remote`, `ces-k64-home`, `fcpq-h16-home`) invert when workers can be
  idle. The inline hand-off mutex is the fastest lock in that world *and* the
  fairest one (`dpq-inline-c256`: Jain 1.000, 0.49 Mops/s, 6 µs bystander p99).
- Caveat on the side experiment: in the sleep world, inline hand-off
  concentrates all client polls on 1–3 workers (`client_poll` share up to 0.90
  on one worker, see `dpq-inline-c256-sleep-sleep-r1.json`), i.e. it exhibits
  the CES-style burden that Q1 penalises; it should be run with the chain
  bound + home break. That is again `ces-pq`.

**Fix.** Make the bystander model a knob (spin/yield, I/O-like sleep) and
report Q1/Q5 for both; state that balancing exists for the spin model. Re-run
the placement recommendation under the sleep model. The Discussion's one
sentence ("with I/O-driven bystanders, idle stealing could rescue trapped
tasks … we have not tested this") understates how much of Q1/Q5 depends on it.

### I5 — weakens-claim: `dispatch` is not tokio's mutex, and the tokio baseline is handicapped by the same harness shape

**Claim affected.** §2.2 "dispatch (tokio, Kotlin, Boost)"; Q4 "the executor
is not a weak baseline: coro `dispatch` runs at 0.90–0.98× `tokio-mutex`".

**Evidence.**

- `tokio::sync::Mutex` is a FIFO semaphore whose release *wakes* the next
  waiter with a permit assigned, and the wake lands in the LIFO slot of the
  unlocking worker (paper §2.4). Coro `dispatch` with `Default` placement puts
  the grantee at the *back* of the unlocker's FIFO (`executor.rs:377-384`);
  with `Inline` it would match tokio's LIFO behaviour, but `Inline` is
  disabled (`dispatch.rs:259-262`). So `dispatch` ≠ tokio's placement, and the
  "0.90–0.98×" agreement is between two differently-placed locks that happen
  to be equally hurt by the synchronous parallel spin (both ~0.21–0.24 Mops/s;
  with a yield they diverge: tokio 0.349, coro `dispatch` 0.215,
  `dispatch-inline` 0.368).
- tokio's LIFO slot is *capped at 3 consecutive* and stealable-after; the
  executor's inline slot is uncapped by design (executor.rs l.34-38). The
  paper says this is deliberate so CES chains are observable; it also means
  `dispatch-inline` would over-state tokio (no cap). A faithful "tokio-like"
  variant needs the cap.
- `tokio-bench` pins tokio worker *i by start order* to CPU i
  (`coro_tokio_baseline/src/workload.rs:13-15`); the coro executor pins by
  worker id. Fine, but the tokio bystander latency is measured by the task
  (from `yield_now()` call to next poll) and includes tokio's defer-list
  semantics, so the tokio bystander p99 (13–19 µs) and coro's (0.8–3 µs) are
  not the same quantity; the paper compares them in Fig. 1/Tab. xrt.

**Fix.** Add `dispatch-inline` with a LIFO cap of 3 as the tokio analogue
and report it beside `dispatch`; label `dispatch` as "FIFO-back hand-off".
Re-run Q4 with the yield harness (I1). Drop cross-runtime bystander-latency
comparisons or measure both the same way.

### I6 — weakens-claim: "lock-agnostic" and "minimal contract" are asserted, not shown

**Claim affected.** Contribution 2 ("a minimal executor contract"), G5,
§4.1.

**Evidence.**

- The contract is three placements plus `reschedule_self_remote`,
  `current_chain`, `current_task_inline_resumed`, `spawn_here`, and
  thread-local `worker_id()` (executor.rs public API, l.236-346). CES needs
  `reschedule_self_remote` (an executor-internal deferred-schedule invariant,
  l.23-29) and `current_chain`; `ces`'s combining attribution needs
  `current_task_inline_resumed`. That is five hooks, three of which tokio does
  not expose and one of which (`Inline` without a cap) tokio would refuse.
  "Only wake placement" is not what the code needs.
- Nothing was ported to a second executor. The tokio comparison uses
  off-the-shelf locks, not the paper's locks on tokio, so "lock-agnostic" and
  "executor-independent" are untested. tokio exposes no `Home` or `Remote`
  placement to a waker, and the paper's own §2.4 shows tokio's LIFO slot is
  already an implicit, capped `Inline`.

**Fix.** Either port `fc-remote` and `dispatch-pq` to tokio (both need only
`Waker::wake` for `Remote`-like behaviour if the injector is acceptable, and
`tokio::task::unconstrained` for the combiner) and show the fairness result
holds there, or scope the contribution to "on an executor exposing
placement".

### I7 — weakens-claim: the starvation clamp is FIFO-degenerate at the default and the fair result depends on a post-hoc clamp

**Claim affected.** G2 ("no request waits more than a fixed number of lock
decisions"), Q2/Q3 results at `c16` (FC-PQ) and `c256`/`c0` (dispatch-pq).

**Evidence.**

- FINDINGS l.17-25 and paper §4.2: at FC-PQ clamp 8 (the code default,
  `fc_pq.rs:42`) the clamp binds and Jain is 0.94; the "fair" 0.997 needs
  clamp 16, at which the worst wait is 17 passes ≈ 272 ops and heavy p99
  626 µs. For `dispatch-pq` the default (16 hand-offs) is FIFO; the fair
  numbers use 256 hand-offs or no clamp. The paper's own Discussion says the
  hand-off clamps "were chosen after an exploratory single-repeat pass showed
  this, not pre-registered". So the fairness headline sits at the one clamp
  setting where G2 is weakest. The bound *is* measured but not reported: in
  `dpqi-dispatch-pq-home-c256-w8-h8-b31-sus-r1.json` `fcpq_wait.max_wait` is
  257 hand-offs, 255 requests were promoted, and 90 769 of 591 608 requests
  (15 %) waited ≥ 63 hand-offs (the histogram's last bucket). At ≈ 7.3 k
  cycles per hand-off (C̄ + o) the worst wait is ≈ 0.85 ms, which exceeds
  every heavy p99 the paper quotes for this lock (685–864 µs).
- The promotion rule itself is sound: `promote_starving` (`fc_pq.rs:362-384`)
  runs after the drain and under the same serialisation as `pop`, so a
  promoted entry has key ≤ every key present and is served in the next tick
  (FC-PQ: within the next pass if ≤ H entries are promoted; `dispatch-pq`:
  at the next hand-off, by arrival among promoted). Measured worst waits of
  17 passes at clamp 16 and 257 hand-offs at clamp 256 agree with that.

**Fix.** Report the worst observed wait (already in `fcpq_wait.max_wait`)
*and* the p99.9 run latency for the fair setting in every fairness table, in
µs as well as ticks; make the clamp a first-class axis in Fig. 3(b–f) for
`dispatch-pq` too (the `todo` on clamps 150–220 is exactly the missing
curve); pre-register the clamp for any re-run.

### I8 — weakens-claim (accounting): burden Jain compares FC/actor pass time with CES closure time; combining cycles include the combiner's own CS

**Claim affected.** Burden Jain and "share of combining cycles per worker"
(Fig. 1d, Fig. 2, Q1, Q5).

**Evidence.**

- `fc.rs:570-576`: `record_combining(w, pass_cycles)` charges the *whole
  pass* (own CS + others' CS + drain + heap + wake issue) to the worker.
- `ces.rs:168-178`: `record_combining(w, dt)` charges only the *closure*
  of an inline-resumed task; queue admin and the wake are excluded
  ("Queue administration is not included", l.24-25). The own CS of the
  chain's *first* link (not inline-resumed) is excluded too.
- `actor.rs:24-28`: charges the pass including drain, like FC.
- So burden Jain is over different quantities per lock family. It changes no
  verdict at 0.125 vs 0.99, but the Q5 actor-vs-FC ratios and any "share of
  combining cycles" figure are not comparable to CES at the ±10 % level, and
  the paper says the charges "come out exactly as for `ces`/`fc`"
  (`actor.rs:27-28`), which is false for `ces`.
- `fc.rs:554-558`: the combiner's own CS is inside `pass_cycles`, so a
  worker whose own client combines a lot is charged for work that would have
  run there anyway. Per-worker *client* poll cycles already capture that.

**Fix.** Define burden as "cycles spent running *other* tasks' closures plus
lock administration" and apply it identically (FC: `pass_cycles − own_cs`,
already computed as `admin` on l.571 but only stored per client; CES: add the
spinlock/handoff cycles). State the definition in §2.3.

### I9 — presentation/accounting: several smaller measurement issues

1. **Warm-up is 200 ms; the CES chain and the actor server placement are
   decided in warm-up** (FINDINGS l.1610-1615: the combiner's bystander "is
   stolen during warm-up and never returns"). The b0 CES results are one
   deterministic outcome of the spawn order (`workload.rs:389-411`: client
   `id % W`, bystander `i % W`). A different seed/spawn order would move which
   worker becomes the combiner but not the 1/W; fine, but say so, and report
   the number of chain starts, not just the median chain length.
2. **`measured_secs` ends when `PHASE_STOP` is stored, but ops are attributed
   by the phase at their *start*** (`workload.rs:322-346`): an op that
   started at `t = 2.000 s` and finished at `2.001 s` is counted, and its
   service cycles are inside the window's CS sum although its CS ran after
   the window. Utilisation can therefore slightly exceed the true window
   share; negligible (≤ 64 ops of ~600 k) but it is the wrong direction for a
   metric that is compared at the 1–3 % level.
3. **"Starved bystander" means one schedule→poll gap ≥ 0.5 × window**
   (`workload.rs:493`, `from_stats`: `count_at_least(threshold)`, drain-time
   poll included). A bystander polled every 0.9 s of a 2 s window counts as
   served, and "no bystander starved" (every table) says nothing about its
   poll rate. Report bystander polls per worker per second beside the p99.
4. **Histogram resolution is 6 %** (`stats.rs:36-42`), and p99 values are
   bucket lower bounds. Many headline latency ratios in FINDINGS (e.g. light
   p99 164 vs 186 µs, "within one bucket") are at the resolution limit. State
   the bucket width beside every latency number or use 32 sub-buckets.
5. **TSC frequency estimate over 100 ms** (`workload.rs:124-130`) gives
   2.2002 GHz; fine, but with CPUs 0–15 capped at 3.0 GHz and 16+ uncapped
   the *non-spin* cost of the same code differs across `W = 16` vs `W = 8`
   cells by ~20 % in wall time while spins are TSC-fixed. The paper attributes
   1.4–3.4 % 09-28→09-29 differences to the cap "[inference]"; a 3.0 GHz cap on
   a 3.7 GHz turbo core is a 19 % change in non-spin time, and the observed
   1.4–3.4 % is consistent with ~15 % of per-op time being non-spin. Say that
   instead of leaving it as inference; it also means the W = 16 cells (CPUs
   8–15 capped, 0–7 capped) are homogeneous but a future W = 32 cell would not
   be.
6. **`service_jain` includes clients with `ops == 0` as zeros** (`jain()`
   ignores nothing); good, but `burden_jain` is `None` for non-combining locks
   and printed as "–", while `actor`'s server-worker share is compared against
   FC's. Consistent, but the paper's Fig. 1(d) "share of combining cycles per
   worker, sorted" for CES uses closure-only cycles (I8).
7. **`dispatch-pq` newcomer init never fires** (FINDINGS l.83-84: "no request
   here took that path") and FC-PQ's fires 64 times in warm-up (Q2). The
   four newcomer options and their CLI/labels are dead weight in the paper;
   cut them or test them with arrivals (I2).
8. **Pre-registration.** H-B was pre-registered and refuted; the fairness
   clamps, the pass limit H = 16, and `home` placement were chosen after
   looking at results (Discussion admits the clamp). The paper should
   separate pre-registered from exploratory findings in one table.

### I10 — presentation: what a sceptical OSDI/EuroSys reviewer will ask for

Ordered by how likely it is to be a rejection reason:

1. **Real application or at least a real critical section** (I3): no
   benchmark with data-dependent CS cost, no locality effect possible.
2. **Open-loop / arrival-driven load and tail latency under load** (I2, I4):
   all clients are closed-loop and always present; no p99.9; no
   latency-vs-load curve; no arrivals.
3. **`ces-pq` / inline hand-off** (I1): the paper names the missing baseline
   itself; a reviewer will not accept "untested" when it is a placement flag.
4. **A second executor** (I6): nothing runs on tokio except tokio's own locks.
5. **Multiple locks per executor and NUMA**: one lock instance, one socket
   (CPUs 0–15 are on socket 0). Combining with two hot locks makes combiner
   burden multiplicative; `Home` placement across sockets changes the
   wake cost. Untested and unmentioned.
6. **Comparison with the obvious tokio-native fair design**: a
   `tokio::sync::Semaphore`-based usage-ordered mutex, or simply
   `dispatch-pq` on tokio. The paper says usage ordering is lock-agnostic;
   show it on the runtime people use.
7. **Is "executor subversion" new?** SCL (2020) defines scheduler subversion
   and its §7 already proposes usage-fair delegation; CES (2025) already
   argues the round-trip point and inline resume; tokio's LIFO slot and
   `async-lock` fairness issues are documented in their trackers. The five
   forms are a taxonomy of known effects; the genuinely new measurements are
   (i) FIFO Jain predicted to three digits from costs and (ii) the placement
   contract fixing combiner burden. Lead with those; do not lead with
   "delegation is the fast realisation" (I1) or with the tokio ratios (I1, I5).
8. **Reproducibility**: no commit id for measured binaries (paper `todo`), a
   `results/` policy that keeps 950 JSONs in git, two clock windows with
   cross-window ratios; state the CPU-frequency cap in the setup, not in a
   footnote.
